# Editorial and visual decisions

Use the smallest graphic that helps the viewer understand the current thought. Select moments from the transcript and actual picture together; silence, emotion and an already clear demonstration often benefit from no added overlay. A graphic should identify, explain, compare or point to evidence. It should not invent a stronger claim than the speaker made.

## Treatments

| Need | Suitable treatment | Decision to verify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First meaningful speaker appearance | Name and brief verified role; usually two lines | Do not infer credentials or show the ID on every cut |
| A specific interface action or passage | Relevant screenshot crop, outlined target/marker, optional magnified inset | Preserve context; highlight the exact supplied text/control |
| One important spoken phrase | Short phrase, underline or marker reveal | Preserve wording/meaning; leave ordinary subtitles to the caption owner |
| Sequence or process | Reveal steps/nodes in spoken order | Avoid showing an unreadable completed diagram before explaining it |
| Comparison or number | Two states or simple chart/stat card | Retain units, date, denominator and source; label hypothetical examples |
| Topic change | Brief section label | Do not insert a full-screen interruption for every paragraph |

## Restrained default

- Use a consistent type hierarchy, a small palette drawn from the project's brand and one primary emphasis per beat. Reuse a motion vocabulary throughout the episode. Resolve actual fonts locally and keep their licenses with the project.
- Start with approximately 0.25–0.4 seconds for an entrance and 0.2–0.3 for an exit, with gentle ease-out/in. These are adjustable craft defaults, not research-derived timing laws. Hold the content steady while it is read. Avoid constant bobbing, excessive bounce, long zoom crawls and gratuitous glitch/flash effects.
- A typical short speaker ID may hold for about 3–5 seconds, longer if required by the actual copy. Budget reading time separately from animation; a useful first estimate is words/3 seconds plus about 0.7 seconds for orientation. Test at playback speed and on a phone. Never shrink long copy until it technically fits; shorten it or redesign the card.
- Land the emphasis with the relevant spoken phrase and retain the qualification. Reveal screenshot → allow orientation → highlight target → hold → exit. Do not animate unrelated elements simultaneously or make the audience read a paragraph while listening to a different explanation.

## Layout and legibility

Author for the actual displayed frame and final delivery size. Start with roughly 5–10% edge clearance for essential horizontal text, then adjust for the user's platform, caption treatment and observed subject positions. This is a layout starting point, not a universal safe-area specification. Portrait interfaces reserve substantial bottom/right areas; inspect the current destination preview instead of assuming landscape margins apply.

Reserve face, hands/demonstration, existing labels and caption/UI regions in the cue plan. A lower third can sit above the subtitle band or on the opposite side of the speaker. A screenshot can occupy a side panel or an intentionally composed full-screen insert when authorized. If neither remains legible, recommend a different layout; the helper does not detect faces or track moving targets.

Inspect the finished episode for baked graphics before adding a panel. Preserve useful existing treatments; when comparing an approved alternative, replace the occupied panel area while keeping the speaker visible. Check that old text does not peek through at the edges or compete during entrances and exits. Time a task outline or key-phrase reveal to the relevant words, with one emphasis at a time, rather than highlighting the whole screenshot throughout the explanation.

Use a stable opaque/controlled plate behind essential text over moving footage. Aim for at least 4.5:1 text contrast; 3:1 can apply to genuinely large text, but raster export/downscaling changes perceived size. Treat WCAG contrast as useful design guidance here, not a claim of certified video accessibility. Do not communicate meaning only through color: add an outline, underline, label or spatial cue. Avoid flashing treatments; ordinary informative graphics do not need a strobe.

Map highlight rectangles to the actual screenshot pixels **after** crop, contain/letterbox sizing and any reframe. Animate the screenshot and its annotations in a shared coordinate wrapper. A static screenshot's boxes do not track a moving screen recording. Retain citation/context text where it matters and enough original context to prevent misleading quotation. Inspect thin strokes, compression, moiré, low-resolution text and alpha fringes over both dark and light footage.

## Review evidence

Inspect representative entrances, reading holds and exits as moving video, including backward seeks in the composition. Verify every mounted cue, not just the title card. Then review the actual composited passage with sound: a good isolated overlay can obscure a moving face or captions when placed on footage. Recheck the first/last frame around source cuts and review portrait variants separately. Keep human acceptance distinct from technical verification.
